René Descartes

  • René Descartes (31 Mar 1596 – 11 Feb 1650) French philosopher and mathematician, often called the father of modern philosophy. He held that certain knowledge comes from reason and clear and distinct ideas rather than the senses.
  • Descartes' three dreams (10 Nov 1619) While serving as a soldier in Germany, Descartes has three dreams in one night, which he takes as a call to rebuild all of science on a single rational method.
  • Rules for the Direction of the Mind (1628) An unfinished treatise on method, arguing that knowledge is built from intuition and deduction modelled on mathematics. It was published only after his death.
  • Discourse on the Method (1637) Written in French for a general audience, it sets out his method of doubt and first states "I think, therefore I am" as the one truth that cannot be doubted.
  • Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) Doubts everything that can be doubted, then rebuilds knowledge on the cogito, the existence of God and innate ideas. The wax argument claims that bodies are known by the intellect, not the senses. It was published with objections from Hobbes, Gassendi and others.
  • Correspondence with Elisabeth of Bohemia (1643 – 1649) Princess Elisabeth presses Descartes on how an immaterial mind can move a material body, exposing the central difficulty of his mind–body dualism.
  • Principles of Philosophy (1644) A textbook version of his system, giving the Latin form "cogito, ergo sum" and deriving physics from first principles grasped by reason.

Baruch Spinoza

  • Baruch Spinoza (24 Nov 1632 – 21 Feb 1677) Dutch philosopher who carried rationalism furthest, holding that the whole of reality can be deduced from definitions and axioms, and that God and Nature are one substance.
  • Spinoza's excommunication (27 Jul 1656) The Portuguese-Jewish community of Amsterdam issues a cherem against the young Spinoza for his "abominable heresies", freeing him to follow reason wherever it leads.
  • Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect (1662) An unfinished work on method that ranks kinds of knowledge, placing hearsay and vague experience below knowledge reached by reason. Its date of writing is approximate; it was published after his death.
  • Principles of Cartesian Philosophy (1663) A reworking of Descartes' Principles in geometric form, and the only book published under Spinoza's own name in his lifetime.
  • Theologico-Political Treatise (1670) Published anonymously, it reads Scripture as a historical text to be judged by reason, and defends freedom of thought. It was widely condemned.
  • Leibniz visits Spinoza (Nov 1676) Leibniz spends several days with Spinoza in The Hague and reads the manuscript of the Ethics, a meeting of the two great continental rationalists.
  • Ethics (1677) Published after his death, it proceeds in geometric order from definitions and axioms. It distinguishes imagination, reason and intuitive knowledge, and holds that adequate ideas are known with certainty.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

  • Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1 Jul 1646 – 14 Nov 1716) German polymath who co-invented calculus. He distinguished truths of reason from truths of fact and defended innate ideas against Locke.
  • Dissertation on the Art of Combinations (1666) His early dream of an alphabet of human thought, in which all concepts are combined from simple ones and every dispute can be settled by calculation.
  • Meditations on Knowledge, Truth, and Ideas (1684) A short essay that refines Descartes' test of clear and distinct ideas, classifying knowledge as obscure or clear, confused or distinct, and adequate or intuitive.
  • Discourse on Metaphysics (1686) Argues that the complete concept of each substance contains everything that will ever happen to it, so that all true propositions are in principle knowable by reason.
  • New Essays on Human Understanding (1704) A chapter-by-chapter reply to Locke's Essay. To "nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses" he adds "except the intellect itself", and compares the mind to veined marble rather than a blank slate. He withheld it when Locke died; it was published in 1765.
  • Monadology (1714) A compact statement of his metaphysics, resting on two great principles of reasoning: the principle of contradiction and the principle of sufficient reason.
  • Leibniz–Clarke correspondence (1715 – 1716) A debate with Samuel Clarke, a follower of Newton, in which Leibniz uses the principle of sufficient reason to argue against absolute space and time.

John Locke

  • John Locke (29 Aug 1632 – 28 Oct 1704) English philosopher and physician, the founder of British empiricism. He held that the mind begins as a blank slate and that all ideas come from experience.
  • First drafts of the Essay (1671) A discussion among "five or six friends" reaches a deadlock, and Locke decides they must first examine what the human understanding is fit to deal with. He writes the first two drafts of the Essay that year.
  • An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689) Attacks the doctrine of innate ideas and argues that all ideas come from sensation and reflection. It also distinguishes primary from secondary qualities. The title page is dated 1690.
  • Molyneux's problem (1693) William Molyneux asks Locke whether a man born blind, who learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, could tell them apart by sight alone on gaining it. Locke agrees with Molyneux that he could not.
  • Second edition of the Essay (1694) Adds the chapter "Of Identity and Diversity", which grounds personal identity in continuity of consciousness rather than of substance, and includes Molyneux's problem.
  • Controversy with Stillingfleet (1696 – 1699) Bishop Edward Stillingfleet charges that Locke's "way of ideas" undermines knowledge of substance and the Trinity. Locke defends it in a series of public letters.
  • Of the Conduct of the Understanding (1706) Intended as a further chapter of the Essay and published after his death, it gives practical advice on training the mind to reason well from experience.

George Berkeley

  • George Berkeley (12 Mar 1685 – 14 Jan 1753) Irish philosopher and Anglican bishop who pushed Locke's empiricism to immaterialism: to be is to be perceived, and no matter exists apart from minds.
  • Philosophical Commentaries (1707 – 1708) Private notebooks kept as a young fellow of Trinity College Dublin, in which he works out his immaterialism while reading Locke and Malebranche.
  • An Essay towards a New Theory of Vision (1709) Argues that we do not see distance directly, but learn to judge it from experience by linking sight with touch, siding with Molyneux and Locke.
  • A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge (1710) Rejects abstract ideas and Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities, and concludes that the existence of sensible things consists in being perceived: esse est percipi.
  • Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous (1713) A popular restatement of his immaterialism, in which Philonous persuades the materialist Hylas that the idea of matter existing unperceived makes no sense.
  • De Motu (1721) Criticises Newton's absolute space and forces as abstractions beyond experience, treating them as useful mathematical hypotheses rather than real things.
  • The Analyst (1734) Attacks the infinitesimals of Newton's and Leibniz's calculus as "ghosts of departed quantities" that no one can clearly conceive.

David Hume

  • David Hume (7 May 1711 – 25 Aug 1776) Scottish philosopher who took empiricism to sceptical conclusions, arguing that causation, the self and induction cannot be justified by reason or experience.
  • Hume at La Flèche (1735 – 1737) Hume writes most of the Treatise in La Flèche, France, home of the Jesuit college where Descartes had been educated.
  • A Treatise of Human Nature (1739 – 1740) Divides perceptions into impressions and ideas, traces causation to habit, and describes the self as a bundle of perceptions. Hume later said it "fell dead-born from the press".
  • An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748) A shorter recasting of the Treatise. It sets out "Hume's fork" between relations of ideas and matters of fact, and the problem of induction. It closes by telling readers to commit to the flames any book of metaphysics that contains neither.
  • Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779) Published after his death, it argues that experience cannot support the argument from design for the existence of God.
  • Hume wakes Kant from his dogmatic slumber (1783) In the Prolegomena, Kant says Hume's critique of causation first interrupted his "dogmatic slumber". Kant's answer, the Critique of Pure Reason, seeks to reconcile rationalism and empiricism.
