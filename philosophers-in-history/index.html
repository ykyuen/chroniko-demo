Chinese dynasty

  • Xia dynasty (2070 BCE – 1600 BCE) The first dynasty in traditional Chinese history, founded by Yu the Great. Its existence is still debated.
  • Shang dynasty (1600 BCE – 1046 BCE) The earliest dynasty supported by written records, known for oracle bones and bronze casting.
  • Western Zhou (1046 BCE – 771 BCE) The first half of the Zhou dynasty, ruled from Haojing until the capital fell to invaders.
  • Eastern Zhou (770 BCE – 256 BCE) The Zhou court moves east to Luoyang. Covers the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods.
  • Qin dynasty (221 BCE – 206 BCE) The first imperial dynasty, which unified China under Qin Shi Huang and standardised script, weights and measures.
  • Western Han (202 BCE – 9) Founded by Liu Bang with its capital at Chang'an. The Silk Road opens under Emperor Wu.
  • Xin dynasty (9 – 23) A short dynasty founded by Wang Mang, who usurped the Han throne.
  • Eastern Han (25 – 220) Han rule restored by Emperor Guangwu with its capital at Luoyang; paper-making is refined.
  • Cao Wei (220 – 266) The northern state of the Three Kingdoms period, founded by Cao Pi.
  • Shu Han (221 – 263) The southwestern state of the Three Kingdoms period, founded by Liu Bei.
  • Eastern Wu (222 – 280) The southeastern state of the Three Kingdoms period, founded by Sun Quan.
  • Western Jin (266 – 316) Founded by Sima Yan, it briefly reunified China after the Three Kingdoms.
  • Eastern Jin (317 – 420) The Jin court retreats south to Jiankang after losing the north.
  • Northern and Southern dynasties (420 – 589) A period of division between north and south. Northern dynasties: Northern Wei, Eastern Wei, Western Wei, Northern Qi, Northern Zhou. Southern dynasties: Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, Chen. Ended by the Sui reunification.
  • Sui dynasty (581 – 618) Reunified China after nearly four centuries of division and built the Grand Canal.
  • Tang dynasty (618 – 907) A cosmopolitan dynasty known for its poetry, art and the height of the Silk Road.
  • Wu Zhou (690 – 705) The dynasty proclaimed by Wu Zetian, the only woman to rule China as emperor, interrupting the Tang.
  • Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (907 – 979) The era of division after the fall of the Tang. Five short-lived dynasties (Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, Later Zhou) succeeded one another in the north while ten kingdoms ruled the south, until the Northern Song reunified China.
  • Liao dynasty (916 – 1125) A Khitan-led dynasty ruling northern China, Mongolia and Manchuria.
  • Northern Song (960 – 1127) Founded by Zhao Kuangyin with its capital at Kaifeng; known for paper money and movable type.
  • Western Xia (1038 – 1227) A Tangut-led dynasty in northwestern China, destroyed by the Mongols.
  • Jin dynasty (1115 – 1234) A Jurchen-led dynasty that overthrew the Liao and drove the Song south.
  • Southern Song (1127 – 1279) The Song court rules from Lin'an (Hangzhou) after losing the north to the Jin.
  • Yuan dynasty (1271 – 1368) The Mongol-led dynasty proclaimed by Kublai Khan.
  • Ming dynasty (1368 – 1644) The last ethnic Han-led imperial dynasty, which built the Forbidden City and much of the Great Wall.
  • Qing dynasty (1636 – 1912) The Manchu-led last imperial dynasty of China, ended by the Xinhai Revolution.

Eastern philosopher

  • Laozi (571 BCE – 471 BCE) Traditionally regarded as the author of the Tao Te Ching and the founder of Taoism. His dates are traditional and uncertain.
  • Siddhartha Gautama (563 BCE – 483 BCE) The Buddha, founder of Buddhism, who taught the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Way. Some scholars date him about 80 years later.
  • Confucius (551 BCE – 479 BCE) Founder of Confucianism, who taught benevolence, ritual propriety and filial piety. His sayings are collected in the Analects.
  • Sun Tzu (544 BCE – 496 BCE) Military strategist traditionally credited with The Art of War. His dates are traditional.
  • Mozi (470 BCE – 391 BCE) Founder of Mohism, who argued for impartial care, opposed offensive war and criticised Confucian ritual.
  • Shang Yang (390 BCE – 338 BCE) Legalist statesman whose reforms in the state of Qin laid the foundations for its later unification of China.
  • Mencius (371 BCE – 289 BCE) Confucian philosopher, known as the Second Sage, who argued that human nature is innately good.
  • Hui Shi (370 BCE – 310 BCE) Philosopher of the School of Names, known for his paradoxes and his debates with Zhuangzi.
  • Zhuangzi (369 BCE – 286 BCE) Taoist philosopher credited with the Zhuangzi, known for the butterfly dream and his playful scepticism.
  • Xunzi (310 BCE – 238 BCE) Confucian philosopher who argued that human nature is bad and must be shaped by ritual and education.
  • Han Feizi (280 BCE – 233 BCE) Synthesised Legalist thought on law, statecraft and the power of the ruler. A student of Xunzi.
  • Dong Zhongshu (179 BCE – 104 BCE) Han dynasty scholar who promoted Confucianism as the official ideology of the imperial state.
  • Nagarjuna (150 – 250) Indian Buddhist philosopher who founded the Madhyamaka school and its doctrine of emptiness.
  • Asanga (300 – 370) Indian Buddhist philosopher who founded the Yogachara school with his half-brother Vasubandhu. Flourished in the 4th century; dates are approximate.
  • Vasubandhu (316 – 396) Indian Buddhist philosopher of the Abhidharma and Yogachara schools. Flourished in the 4th to 5th century; dates are approximate.
  • Bodhidharma (440 – 528) Semi-legendary monk who brought Chan (Zen) Buddhism to China and is regarded as its first patriarch. Lived in the 5th or 6th century; dates are approximate.
  • Huineng (638 – 713) The Sixth Patriarch of Chan Buddhism, who taught sudden enlightenment. His teachings are recorded in the Platform Sutra.
  • Zhou Dunyi (1017 – 1073) Song dynasty pioneer of Neo-Confucianism, author of the Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate.
  • Zhu Xi (18 Oct 1130 – 23 Apr 1200) Synthesised Neo-Confucianism. His commentaries on the Four Books became the basis of the imperial examinations.
  • Lu Jiuyuan (1139 – 1192) Neo-Confucian who argued that the mind is principle, debating Zhu Xi. Founder of the School of Mind.
  • Wang Yangming (26 Oct 1472 – 9 Jan 1529) Ming dynasty Neo-Confucian who taught the unity of knowledge and action and innate knowing.

Western events

  • First Olympic Games (776 BCE) The traditional date of the first Olympic Games, held at Olympia in Greece in honour of Zeus.
  • Founding of the Roman Republic (509 BCE) The Romans overthrow their last king and found a republic governed by consuls and the Senate.
  • Foundation of Athenian Democracy (508 BCE) The reforms of Cleisthenes lay the foundations of direct democracy by the citizens of Athens.
  • The Greco-Persian Wars (499 BCE – 449 BCE) The Greek city-states resist invasion by the Persian Empire, with battles at Marathon, Thermopylae and Salamis.
  • The Peloponnesian War (431 BCE – 404 BCE) Athens and its empire fight the Peloponnesian League led by Sparta, ending in Athenian defeat.
  • Conquests of Alexander the Great (334 BCE – 323 BCE) Alexander conquers the Persian Empire and builds an empire from Greece to northwestern India, beginning the Hellenistic age.
  • Assassination of Julius Caesar (15 Mar 44 BCE) Caesar is stabbed to death by Roman senators on the Ides of March, sparking a new round of civil wars.
  • Rise of the Roman Empire (16 Jan 27 BCE) Octavian is granted the title Augustus by the Senate and becomes the first Roman emperor.
  • Crucifixion of Jesus (30) Jesus is crucified in Jerusalem under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, in about AD 30 or 33.
  • Fall of the Western Roman Empire (4 Sep 476) Odoacer deposes Romulus Augustulus, the last Western Roman emperor.
  • Beginning of the Holy Roman Empire (2 Feb 962) Otto I is crowned emperor. Some date the empire from the coronation of Charlemagne in 800 instead.
  • The Great Schism (16 Jul 1054) The churches of Rome and Constantinople excommunicate each other, splitting Christianity into Catholic and Eastern Orthodox.
  • Norman Conquest of England (28 Sep 1066 – 25 Dec 1066) William, Duke of Normandy, lands in England, wins the Battle of Hastings and is crowned king on Christmas Day.
  • Signing of the Magna Carta (15 Jun 1215) King John of England agrees to the Magna Carta under pressure from his barons, limiting royal power.
  • The Hundred Years' War (24 May 1337 – 19 Oct 1453) England and France fight on and off for over a century over the French throne; Joan of Arc is among its famous figures.
  • The Black Death (1346 – 1353) A bubonic plague pandemic kills an estimated third or more of Europe's population.
  • The Renaissance (1400 – 1600) A cultural movement starting in Italy that revived classical learning, with figures such as Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo. Its dates are approximate.
  • Gutenberg Invents the Printing Press (1440) Johannes Gutenberg invents the movable-type printing press, spreading books and knowledge quickly across Europe.
  • Fall of Constantinople (29 May 1453) Ottoman forces capture Constantinople, ending the Byzantine Empire.
  • Columbus Reaches the Americas (12 Oct 1492) Christopher Columbus lands in the Bahamas on his first voyage, opening the Americas to European colonisation.
  • The Protestant Reformation (31 Oct 1517 – 1648) Martin Luther publishes his Ninety-five Theses, challenging papal authority and giving rise to Protestantism.
  • The Scientific Revolution Begins (1543) Copernicus publishes his heliocentric model, opening the era of modern science based on observation and experiment.
  • The Thirty Years' War & Peace of Westphalia (23 May 1618 – 24 Oct 1648) A religious and political war fought mainly in Central Europe, ended by the Peace of Westphalia, which established the principle of state sovereignty.
  • The Glorious Revolution (5 Nov 1688 – 13 Feb 1689) James II is deposed in favour of William III and Mary II, laying the foundations of constitutional monarchy.
  • The First Industrial Revolution (1760 – 1840) Mechanised manufacturing, starting in Britain, transforms economies and societies through the steam engine and textile machinery.
  • The American Revolution (1765 – 1789) The Thirteen Colonies rebel against British rule, declare independence and create the United States.
  • The French Revolution (5 May 1789 – 9 Nov 1799) A period of political upheaval that ends the French monarchy under the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity.
  • Romanticism (1800 – 1850) An artistic and intellectual movement stressing emotion, imagination and nature, in reaction to the Enlightenment and industrialisation. Its dates are approximate.
  • Napoleonic Wars & Congress of Vienna (18 May 1803 – 20 Nov 1815) Napoleon's France fights a series of European coalitions until his defeat at Waterloo; the Congress of Vienna redraws the map of Europe.
  • Springtime of the Peoples (Jan 1848 – Oct 1849) Also known as the Revolutions of 1848, a wave of revolutions across Europe demanding liberal reform and national independence.
  • Unification of Germany & Italy (1859 – 18 Jan 1871) Cavour and Garibaldi drive the unification of Italy, while Bismarck founds the German Empire after the Franco-Prussian War.
  • The American Civil War (12 Apr 1861 – 26 May 1865) A civil war between the Union and the Confederacy, ending in Union victory and the abolition of slavery.
  • Berlin Conference (15 Nov 1884 – 26 Feb 1885) European powers set the rules for dividing Africa, accelerating the Scramble for Africa.
  • World War I (28 Jul 1914 – 11 Nov 1918) A global war centred in Europe between the Allies and the Central Powers, causing tens of millions of casualties.
  • The Russian Revolution (8 Mar 1917 – 7 Nov 1917) The February Revolution topples the Tsar, and the Bolsheviks seize power in the October Revolution.
  • Treaty of Versailles (28 Jun 1919) The peace treaty ending World War I, imposing harsh reparations and territorial losses on Germany.
  • Wall Street Crash & The Great Depression (24 Oct 1929 – 1939) The Wall Street stock market crash triggers a worldwide depression, with soaring unemployment and poverty.
  • Rise of Nazi Germany (30 Jan 1933) Adolf Hitler is appointed Chancellor of Germany, and the Nazi Party quickly establishes a dictatorship.
  • World War II (1 Sep 1939 – 2 Sep 1945) A global war between the Allies and the Axis powers, the deadliest conflict in human history.
  • Germany invaded Poland (1 Sep 1939 – 6 Oct 1939) Germany invades Poland, and Britain and France declare war, starting World War II in Europe.
  • The Holocaust (1941 – 1945) Nazi Germany and its collaborators systematically murder around six million European Jews.
  • Operation Barbarossa (22 Jun 1941 – 5 Dec 1941) Germany invades the Soviet Union in the largest land offensive in history.
  • Normandy Landings (6 Jun 1944) Allied forces land in Normandy on D-Day, opening a second front in Western Europe.
  • Victory in Europe (8 May 1945) Germany surrenders unconditionally, ending World War II in Europe.
  • Atomic Bombings of Hiroshima & Nagasaki (6 Aug 1945 – 9 Aug 1945) The United States drops atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the only use of nuclear weapons in war.

Western philosopher

  • Thales of Miletus (626 BCE – 548 BCE) Pre-Socratic philosopher regarded as the father of Western philosophy, who held that the first principle of all things is water.
  • Heraclitus (535 BCE – 475 BCE) Pre-Socratic philosopher who stressed that everything is in flux, famously saying that no one can step into the same river twice. His dates are approximate.
  • Parmenides (515 BCE – 450 BCE) Founder of the Eleatic school, who argued that being is one and unchanging, and that change is an illusion of the senses. His dates are approximate.
  • Socrates (470 BCE – 399 BCE) Athenian philosopher who sought truth through questioning dialogue and was eventually sentenced to death. He left no writings; his thought is known mainly through Plato.
  • Plato (428 BCE – 348 BCE) A student of Socrates who founded the Academy in Athens, proposed the theory of Forms and wrote the Republic.
  • Aristotle (384 BCE – 322 BCE) A student of Plato and tutor of Alexander the Great, whose works span logic, ethics, politics and the natural sciences.
  • Epicurus (341 BCE – 270 BCE) Founder of Epicureanism, who held that the goal of life is pleasure in the form of tranquillity and freedom from pain.
  • Zeno of Citium (334 BCE – 262 BCE) Founder of Stoicism, who taught living in accordance with reason and nature, with virtue as the only good.
  • Seneca the Younger (4 BCE – 65) Roman Stoic philosopher, playwright and statesman who advised the emperor Nero and was later forced to take his own life.
  • Marcus Aurelius (26 Apr 121 – 17 Mar 180) Roman emperor and Stoic philosopher, author of the Meditations.
  • Anselm of Canterbury (1033 – 21 Apr 1109) Archbishop of Canterbury and scholastic philosopher who proposed the ontological argument for the existence of God.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225 – 7 Mar 1274) The great synthesiser of scholasticism, who united Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology and wrote the Summa Theologica.
  • William of Ockham (1287 – Apr 1347) Medieval scholastic philosopher known for Ockham's razor: entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity.
  • Francis Bacon (22 Jan 1561 – 9 Apr 1626) English philosopher and statesman who championed an empirical scientific method based on induction, famous for "knowledge is power".
  • René Descartes (31 Mar 1596 – 11 Feb 1650) The father of modern philosophy, who proposed "I think, therefore I am" and founded analytic geometry.
  • John Locke (29 Aug 1632 – 28 Oct 1704) English empiricist who described the mind as a blank slate. His ideas of natural rights and the social contract laid the foundations of liberalism.
  • Spinoza (24 Nov 1632 – 21 Feb 1677) Dutch rationalist philosopher who held that God is Nature, author of the Ethics.
  • Leibniz (1 Jul 1646 – 14 Nov 1716) German rationalist philosopher and mathematician who proposed the theory of monads and invented calculus independently of Newton.
  • Voltaire (21 Nov 1694 – 30 May 1778) French Enlightenment thinker and writer who championed religious tolerance, freedom of speech and the separation of church and state.
  • David Hume (7 May 1711 – 25 Aug 1776) Scottish empiricist known for his scepticism about causation and induction.
  • Rousseau (28 Jun 1712 – 2 Jul 1778) Geneva-born Enlightenment thinker, author of The Social Contract and Emile, who deeply influenced the French Revolution.
  • Immanuel Kant (22 Apr 1724 – 12 Feb 1804) German philosopher, author of the Critique of Pure Reason, who reconciled rationalism and empiricism and proposed the categorical imperative.
  • Hegel (27 Aug 1770 – 14 Nov 1831) German idealist who used dialectics to explain the development of history and spirit, author of the Phenomenology of Spirit.
  • John Stuart Mill (20 May 1806 – 7 May 1873) English philosopher and economist, a leading figure of utilitarianism and author of On Liberty.
  • Kierkegaard (5 May 1813 – 11 Nov 1855) Danish philosopher and theologian regarded as the father of existentialism, who stressed individual choice and faith.
  • Karl Marx (5 May 1818 – 14 Mar 1883) German philosopher and economist who developed historical materialism, co-wrote The Communist Manifesto with Engels and wrote Capital.
  • Nietzsche (15 Oct 1844 – 25 Aug 1900) 德國哲學家，宣告「上帝已死」，提出超人與權力意志等概念，著有《查拉圖斯特拉如是說》。 German philosopher who declared that "God is dead", proposed the Übermensch and the will to power, and wrote Thus Spoke Zarathustra.
  • Edmund Husserl (8 Apr 1859 – 27 Apr 1938) 德國哲學家，現象學的創始人。 German philosopher, the founder of phenomenology.
  • Bertrand Russell (18 May 1872 – 2 Feb 1970) British philosopher, logician and mathematician, a founder of analytic philosophy, awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950.
  • Ludwig Wittgenstein (26 Apr 1889 – 29 Apr 1951) Austrian-British philosopher, author of the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus and Philosophical Investigations, deeply influential in the philosophy of language.
  • Martin Heidegger (26 Sep 1889 – 26 May 1976) German philosopher, author of Being and Time, who inquired into the meaning of being.
  • Jean-Paul Sartre (21 Jun 1905 – 15 Apr 1980) French existentialist philosopher and writer who held that existence precedes essence, and declined the 1964 Nobel Prize in Literature.
  • Simone de Beauvoir (9 Jan 1908 – 14 Apr 1986) French existentialist philosopher and writer, author of The Second Sex and a key figure of feminism.
  • Albert Camus (7 Nov 1913 – 4 Jan 1960) French writer and philosopher who explored the absurd, author of The Stranger and The Myth of Sisyphus, awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1957.
